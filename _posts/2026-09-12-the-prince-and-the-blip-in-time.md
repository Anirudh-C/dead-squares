The author hopes that the spectacle of this game is carried over in the ensuing description<span style="background-color:red; color:white">??</span>. The game that is the subject of this post is the game between Magnus Carlsen and Alireza Firouzja in the penultimate round of the group stages of the Global Chess League 2026. While there were some consequences of this game to the overall tournament, they are of no consequence to us, the author and the (hopefully) eager reader. # A Mild Surprise and a Slight Chink
Carlsen with the white pieces (and a mountain of confidence) begins the game with **1. d4**, assuming of course that Firouzja would resort to the *King's Indian Defense*, as he has on many an occasion in the past. For the benefit of the reader, the *King's Indian Defense* opens with the moves **1. d4 Nf6 2. c4 g6 3. Nc3 Bg7** yielding a position that looks as such

The author, in fact, plays the *King's Indian Defense* extensively, primarily due to the fact that it is a *setup*-based open. That is, immaterial of white's moves, black may play **1. .. Nf6 2. .. g6 3. .. Bg7** followed by the immediate castling of the black king. Of course, under threat of physical harm by white, black must preferably run away and avoid playing the *King's Indian Defense*. 

Instead, Firouzja opted for the *Grunfeld Defense*, which occurred in this game with the move **3. .. d5**, which evoked the surprise of the commentators, the author[^3] and potentially Carlsen.

Carlsen quashing all manner of doubt, regarding his potential surprise, proceeded to play in the manner that is characteristic of him for the past 15 years. The mildly impatient author would like to turn the reader's attention to the following position that occurred after the moves **3. Nc3 d5 4. cxd5 Nxd5 5. e4 Nxc3 6. bxc3 Bg7 7. Be3 c5 8. Qd2 Qa5 9. Rc1 O-O 10. Nf3 Rd8 11. Be2 Bg4 12. d5 Bxf3 13. gxf3 c4 14. h4 e6 15. Bxc4**.

In this position, Firouzja used his powers of psychic interference to look into the author's mind (all the way back in the audience) and play the author's intended move **15. .. Nc6**. This move, while being a blatant act of plagiarism, is also an attempt to develop black's final minor piece and connect the rooks. The notion of connecting the rooks, yields stability to the position as the rooks now support each other and moreover, they add a layer of protection to the black king. No major white piece (rooks and queen) may waltz into the position and harass the black king! In addition, the move adds an air of cheekiness to the position, as Firouzja says "Oh no! My knight!". The observant reader may note that Carlsen is unlikely to capture the knight on c6 as his queen would be under attack by the black rook on d8.

While the move seems to be cheeky, yet beneficial, black's position is in fact fairly uncomfortable. While Carlsen's king is in the center, there is no means to attack it: there are no open lines. Carlsen's king side pawns have been torn apart (observe that the f2 pawn is stuck behind a pawn on f3 and there is no g-pawn), but Carlsen's king is very safe currently in the center. Firouzja has his queen on a5 and his bishop on g7 both trained on the pawn on c3, but the pawn is sufficiently defended. If Firouzja could manage to distract Carlsen for long enough to teleport his knight to a4<span style="background-color:teal; color:white">!!</span>, then the pawn on c3 would be weak, with ideas of putting a rook on c8 to increase the pressure. However, *Prince Ali* (Firouzja) noticed that this move created a very specific weakness in Carlsen's position and in swift fashion played the extremely resilient move **16. .. Qa4**. The idea is that Carlsen's move **16. f4**, resulted in a weakening of the e4-pawn. The queen now attacks the bishop on c4 and if the bishop were to move, the e4-pawn as well. The loss of the weak pawn on e4 would have exposed Carlsen's king and simultaneously attacking the lonely white rook on h1<span style="background-color:teal; color:white">!!</span>.

# Firouzja does Firouzja things
However, after spending *three and a half* minutes, Carlsen played the move **17. Qd3** in the hopes of also keeping an eye on the c3 pawn. 
<chess-board
    orientiation="white"
    position="r2r2k1/pp3pbp/2n1p1p1/3P4/q1B1PP1P/2PQB3/P4P2/2R1K2R b K - 2 17"
    style="width: 60%; margin-inline: auto;">
</chess-board>
Firouzja pounced on the white queen side and played the move **17. .. Na5**, occupying the square recently vacated by the queen. And now, the the pressure on the c3-pawn is real<span style="background-color:teal; color:white">!!</span>. The rook on a7 now exhibits the sinister intentions of moving to c8 to further increase this pressure! Moreover, the bishop on c4 is also attacked twice! Firouzja unleashes a devastating attack and starts asking some real questions of Carlsen. 
<chess-board
    orientiation="white"

The author notes that the all-knowing and all-seeing engine evaluates this position to be ever so marginally better for Carlsen who has the advantage of *0.2 pawns*, whatever that means... Any biological player, of course, can note the difficulty in managing this position under time pressure. 

At this stage, the author notes that the energy of the commentators has left the audience on the edge of their seats. Everyone cranes their necks with their breaths held, in anticipation of the seemingly foreign notion of Magnus Carlsen being under the gun; that too, against the prince, as annointed by Carlsen himself! Of course, the author and the masses maintained their silence, while being reminded by the staff holding up the ominous boards of *"Maintain Silence During the Games"*[^4]. The tension in the vast playing hall was palpable with all eyes trained on the two icons who have a combined total of around *12 minutes* to determine the outcome of the game. With careful consideration, the moves **18. Bb5 Qa3** are swiftly played, with the wiseacre and unimaginative engine suggesting that this position (illustrated below) is dead drawn...

Carlsen now heads into a deep think to identify what to do with this position and the chink in the armour widens: Carlsen plays **19. Qd1**. To the author, it seems that this is a bad move as it seems to lose the c3-pawn, which to the naive mind of the author, was the pressure point of the position. Carlsen now has little over 3 minutes to play as many moves it requires to survive this game and must make these moves against arguably one of the best speed chess players on the planet. 

The author admits that the intensity of the position and the atmosphere in the playing hall was getting too much to bear. The excited voices of the commentators and the wriggling of the author's neighbours did not do much to aid in these feelings. The author had to make sure to avoid falling off his chair at this stage, a noise that was sure to illict angry hushes from the organisers and potentially disgruntled looks from the players themselves. Nonetheless, given that the author's focus had wavered, the rest of the game was gone in the blink of the eye. By move 23 (4 moves after the last explicit update given above), the players had a minute and a half each. Firouzja played with a monstrous energy and enthusiasm, having reached what was an extremely comfortable time situation for him. Firouzja began his dance of routing his pieces at lightnight speed with a demonic accuracy. Just as the game finished swiftly, as does the description of the game.
